              North Couty Times March 24, 2002
               Do North County
                    conservatives and liberals
                    really want to discuss the
                    issues

                    Mark Thornhill
                    Commentary

                    Last month the North County Times ran a story about a continuing series of
                    programs at Palomar College that ostensibly seeks to bring conservatives and
                    liberals in the community together to discuss the topic of gender identity (gay,
                    lesbian, bisexual and transgender issues). The professor organizing the programs
                    says he is concerned that it isn't safe to be different in North County and that
                    what's needed is "SpeakOut" events to educate the public about "a quiet
                    undercurrent of prejudice against gays and lesbians on our campus and in the
                    community at large."

                    The phrase "quiet undercurrent of prejudice" caught my eye.

                    Suppose one holds to traditional moral values and disagrees with some aspects of
                    the gay-rights movement's political agenda. Is that person automatically
                    considered prejudiced? If so, then can there ever be a meeting of the minds on
                    gender identity issues?

                    The SpeakOut programs at Palomar this year include plays, films and forums that
                    are, to put it mildly, sympathetic to the interests of people with alternative
                    lifestyles. One play is a black comedy that ridicules parenthood.

                    Another play will be "an exploration of the fate of those judged different by Hitler
                    and Nazi Germany." A mixed-media event examines the word "queer." A film,
                    "Boys Don't Cry", will look at the problem of gender identity crisis. And a forum
                    and "free-speech zone" will be opportunities for participants to speak their minds
                    about gender identity topics.

                    Something was missing, however.

                    There was no mention of any events presenting the traditional moral perspective
                    regarding gender identity.

                    The professor organizing SpeakOut, Michael Mufson, said he saw the forum as "a
                    safe platform for diverse voices to speak openly about the issues that divide us."
                    He said he expected heterosexuals and religious people to bring their perspectives
                    to the forum.

                    Those expectations were not realized.

                    I attended the March 3 forum. As I took a seat in Howard Brubeck theater on the
                    Palomar campus, I surveyed the audience. One hundred or more people came. Of
                    those who expressed opinions, the vast majority were sympathetic to liberal views
                    regarding gender identity (this was, after all, a college campus). I was of a very
                    small minority ---- a member of the non-Palomar community who holds culturally
                    conservative values regarding homosexuality (by culturally conservative values I
                    do not mean hateful values; I mean values that acknowledge we are all God's
                    children and that we should find ways to get along without compromising our
                    moral principles).

                    Halfway through the discussion, Professor Mufson, perhaps sensing that no
                    conservative voices were being advanced, asked the audience, "Are there any
                    heterosexual homophobes out there who want to express an opinion?" Several
                    times he asked for "homophobes" to join in.

                    I was struck speechless. "Homophobe" is a derogatory term. For the professor to
                    use that term to intimidate conservatives ---- especially after a discussion about
                    how gender-different people decry the use of the "f" word ---- was appalling.

                    I asked Mufson later why he addressed conservatives as blanket homophobes.

                    Who in the audience, I asked, would feel safe to respond when labeled so
                    unfairly? Mufson said he was sorry that the term prevented me from speaking at
                    the forum but said he was using it "tongue-in-cheek."

                    (Memo to conservatives: It's OK to use derogatory terms as long as you use them
                    tongue-in-cheek.)

                    Something else at the forum struck me as remarkable. Several of Mufson's
                    students attending the event were not exactly politically correct regarding
                    homosexual issues and they said so. Yet they were quick to point out that they
                    weren't being judgmental ---- just uncomfortable with parts of the stereotypical
                    gay lifestyle. And so, by appealing to a sense that morality is subjective and a
                    matter of preference, they were effectively marginalized.

                    Can a dialogue about gender identity issues between conservatives and liberals in
                    North County ever get off the ground if conservatives are intimidated with
                    derogatory labels, suspected of being prejudiced just for disagreeing, or
                    marginalized because it is politically incorrect to express judgment?

                    I did not go to Palomar thinking that the forum would be an exercise in balance. I
                    knew the deck would be stacked to some degree. And I know that if the forum
                    had been held at a church, synagogue or mosque, and had been sponsored by a
                    culturally conservative organization, the dialogue would have been markedly
                    different. Still, I hoped there would be some effort to provide a "safe platform"
                    for cultural conservatives to outline to liberals their reasons for holding their views
                    ---- especially a platform that is supported in part by my tax dollars.

                    Palomar College, in its sponsorship of SpeakOut, constructed a facade to mask
                    politically correct indoctrination. The college is advertising the events as
                    community outreach: an opportunity for "diverse voices to speak openly about the
                    issues that divide us."

                    That's false advertising. If Palomar truly wants to include voices from cultural
                    conservatives ---- who are a majority in North County ---- it needs to take
                    SpeakOut back to the drawing board.

                    Mark Thornhill is editorial cartoonist for the North County Times.

                    3/24/02


North County Times March 29, 2002
The other side of Thornhill's
                    coin

                    Mark Adams
                    Commentary

                    My wife and I were members of the audience at the March 3 "SpeakOut" event at
                    Palomar College. I would like to respond to Mark Thornhill's article in the March
                    24 Perspective section: "Can we talk?"

                    I was the person who pointed out that an African-American member of the group
                    had the privilege to ask that a painful prejudicial word not be used but that a
                    homosexual would not be given that ability. I was baffled that in a discussion of
                    prejudice and gender oppression, such an example was thought to be beyond
                    political correctness.

                    I was also disappointed to hear from students that they use derogatory terms for
                    homosexuality matter-of-factly ---- although not to a gay person's face.
                    Oppression, whether it is directed at a member of the group it refers to or used to
                    deride someone else, is harmful to us as a society. I am against censoring any
                    language. Prohibiting words adds to their power. I favor pointing out the intent of
                    their use and feel there is accountability for the effect they have.

                    Excuse me: I seem to have actually presented an opinion on the issues of the
                    forum, unlike Thornhill, who wrapped himself in the mantle of "traditional,
                    culturally conservative, moral values." I'd say that many of the 35 to 40 who
                    attended held views similar to his. Although I describe myself as liberal, my
                    beliefs come from a traditional source of culturally inclusive morals. Thornhill
                    seems to feel those adjectives are the property of conservatives. Isn't that a
                    prejudice?

                    As to professor Mufson's call for "heterosexual homophobes" to speak, I recall
                    that I and others immediately and loudly called him on his incorrectness. There
                    was a clear recognition of his taunt and discussion of his purpose in doing it.

                    The overall tone of the forum was friendly and open.

                    Thornhill stated he "knew the deck would be stacked to some degree." I would
                    say he arrived with his own cards stacked and that his own prejudices against an
                    open forum on gender issues precluded him from participating. (I am offended by
                    his statement, "This was, after all, a college campus.") If the event had been
                    hosted by a religious institution, I doubt that many of the participants would
                    consider it a "safe platform" for their views. Thornhill's opinion, understood so
                    well it need not be defined any further than "traditional moral values," is clearly
                    supported by the majority of North County.

                    Thornhill's article reached tens of thousands more people than the SpeakOut did,
                    or any of the events "sympathetic to the interest of people with alternative
                    lifestyle" will. I hope the space he received in this newspaper alleviates the
                    marginalization he felt when he questioned whether "it is politically incorrect to
                    express judgment." His opinion would have been welcome at the SpeakOut.
                    Perhaps he realized he had only judgments to offer and therefore remained silent.

                    Everyday life is a constant platform for Thornhill's views. Our democracy,
                    formed by the majority of voters, is based on the protection of diverse opinion.
                    How is it that we look the other way when it involves people from whom we
                    differ?

                    The forum and the events are attempting to address the prejudice, oppression,
                    hate and harm that homosexuals have to deal with, even though they have the
                    protection of the law as U.S. citizens. I invite anyone of any opinion to join the
                    online discussion board at www.palomar.edu/speakout/.

                    Mark Adams lives in Escondido.

The day I lost my innocence
Brittany Torgerson
Commentary
What's to come of the world we live in? I think back to the times of the Cold War or the Cuban missile crisis and the fear that people had of nuclear war. Before Sept. 11, I would laugh when I watched movies that showed the nuclear war drills that children had to do in school, as if hiding under a desk would save them from atomic bombs.

Now I feel I understand the paranoia that was widespread. I hear news about upcoming terrorist attacks, or the threat of nuclear war, and I feel panic. Am I safe anymore? Was I a fool to believe I ever really was?
 

My dislike of this war has turned into a deep hatred of our political leaders. Does it make me un-American to think this way? I want to express my disgust to the world but my fear of being targeted by Big Brother keeps my lips shut. Should I then close my eyes to what is happening around me?

More than anything I want to rewind the clock back to a day when I felt good about living in this country. How contradictory, to think that ignorance is bliss. The rock band Rage Against the Machine declared, "If ignorance is bliss, then knock the smile off my face." When I listen to that verse, I bob my head in agreement, but doesn't that make me a big hypocrite?

Every time I read about the war or watch the news, I am confused about what to believe and what to disregard as propaganda. Will we ever receive the whole truth and nothing but, or am I just dreaming of my version of a perfect world?

Is it wrong to expect our government officials to tell us the truth? What about all that talk about "of the people, by the people and for the people"? Was that just pish-posh to make us content?

I don't understand how our involvement in the Middle East is protecting my interests. The only interest that is being protected is the deep pockets of the oil companies. I don't understand why it is OK for our military to kill thousands of Middle Easterners, but when they finally retaliate we say, "How dare you?"

I do not condone what happened on Sept 11, but how cocky of us to think we are untouchable.

Now I am bombarded with images of evil when there is also evil in our own government.

Why aren't more people seeing this ugly truth? Have we been so brainwashed by consumerism that we can't see the lies anymore? And we wonder why so many other countries view Americans as stupid.

My faith in my government and fellow citizens has plummeted so far in the past nine months that I don't think it will ever return. But at least my eyes are open now.

Brittany Torgerson of Oceanside is a student at Palomar College.

North County Times September 8, 2002
Mourn, but stay steadfast
J. Stryker Meyer
North County Times
This week dozens of memorials will be held across North County and America to remember one of the most tragic days in our history.

There are so many planned in North County this week, it's difficult to decide which one to attend. From the shores of Oceanside to Battery Park in New York City and the Pentagon in Virginia, our fellow citizens will recall the tragedy of 9/11 and the thousands of stories of heroism of men and women who rose far above the call of duty to serve their country that day.
 

As a combat veteran and as a reporter, I am concerned about our country.

First, I detest the lack of resolution -- especially now that the silly season is upon us ---- by certain members of Congress: the harping, complaining and nagging of President Bush as he pursues the criminals who launched and funded the heinous 9/11 attacks.

Everyone in our country should remain steadfast throughout the long and uncertain course this war will run. Unconventional warfare against an elusive and cowardly enemy who attacks only civilian targets is a novel and deadly concept that we are still coming to grips with.

Having served in Vietnam, I remember watching with chagrin as the peace movement gathered steam in the United States while I was one of 3.4 million Americans were fighting a deadly and dedicated enemy. I found it ironic that those in the peace movement claimed to be campaigning to end the war while calling to bring home the veterans from Southeast Asia, yet when veterans returned, many had garbage thrown on them by their fellow citizens.

In this war against terrorism, the national will to execute it to the fullest extent, with or without allies, must not falter. If the campaign wilts or is thwarted by political shortsightedness, then this country will be attacked again, possibly in a more devastating fashion.

Yet as much as I bitterly disagreed with those in the peace movement, I felt that the First Amendment guaranteed their right to protest. In a strange way, I drew comfort from the fact that I lived in a country where our freedom permitted such polarized and diverse opinions to flourish.

That's what makes America great.

However, as a reporter who chaffs against government secrecy, I am concerned about the erosion of some basic civil rights and the lack of accountability in some government agencies. I care a great deal about homeland security, but I am also worried about ludicrous policies that generate horrendous lines at airports because the civil servants are poorly trained and given mindless instructions.

Those policies do nothing to halt terrorism. They do inconvenience thousands of citizens every day.

The federal government is fighting the effort to arm the pilots of American airplanes while rushing through a multimillion-dollar campaign to arm air marshals ---- a program fraught with problems and ineptitude. A large percentage of pilots are veterans, many of whom trained in Air Force, Navy, Marine or Army aviation programs. Most of our pilots are bright, dedicated and fearless.

On 9/11, I shall mourn the tragedies that slammed our country one year ago. And I will pray that our civil rights will remain in place, unsullied by government.

J. Stryker Meyer is a North County Times staff writer.
