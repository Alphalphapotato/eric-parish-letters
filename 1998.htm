Oceanside North County Times   February 5, 1998
Horn uses tactics of intimidation
Editor:
 I must disagree with Supervisor Bill Horn (Jan. 29). In his opinion piece, he laments the representation by public defender Steve Carroll of Posole ex-gang member David Englebrecht in his civil case against the court injunction leveled against gang members in their Eastside neighborhood. Someday, hopefully, the courts will view these kind of restrictions for what they truly are - amazing violations of the Bill of Rights. Since when is the wearing of certain clothes, carrying pagers and cell phones, or visiting friends deemed illegal acts and grounds for arrest? And this only applies to the Eastside area? Nowhere else? I find it unbelievable what our society will do to attain "security" against "them".
 So Mr. Horn calls Mr. Carroll to his office to "simply exercise my civil right to express my displeasure"? Then he gets the Board of Supervisors to examine the action in closed session. No intimidation meant, I am sure. Then Mr. Horn gets a big opinion piece printed in the North County Times. All of this because someone has the guts to challenge a public lynching? Perhaps Mr. Horn should concentrate on getting more checkpoints in Oceanside to catch all those criminals with broken turn signals.
                                                                                 ERIC PARISH

 Oceanside North County Times   October 23, 1998
Hate-crime laws are justified
      ERIC PARISH
Community Forum
 Burning a cross on a black family's yard or spray painting swastikas on a Jewish temple have been classified as hate crimes for many years now. Hate crimes have usually been defined on the basis of race,religion, or national origin.
 Now we have the death of Matthew Shepard and a new federal hate crime bill being debated in Congress that would add sexual orientation to that list. As a result, we have seen an explosion of  antagonism toward the idea of hate crime laws in general. Fueled by the religious right, the public is being bombarded by the idea that the hate crime statutes already on our books are "thought crime" statutes.
 Not at all true. Hate crime laws are called "sentence-enhancing" laws. If you beat me up, it might be third degree assault. If you beat me up because I am white, then as a hate crime, you might be  sentenced  for second- or first-degree assault.
 That's right, hate crimes apply to white, Christian heterosexual males also. Otherwise, it would be unconstitutional, under the 14th Amendment.
 The Supreme Court upheld hate-crime laws in 1993 in Wisconsin v. Mitchell. Mitchell, a black youth convicted of assault while stealing the tennis shoes of a white teenager, took his case all the way to the Wisconsin Supreme Court. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled on appeal that the Wisconsin hate-crime statute was constitutional in using motive as a criterion in sentencing.
 Judges frequently use the motive of a crime in deciding the sentence. That also is the purpose of a hate crime law in the first place. Justice William Rehnquist took note of the complaint that hate-crime laws might have a chilling effect on free speech rights. His response:  "The prospect of a citizen suppressing his bigoted beliefs for fear that evidence of those beliefs will be introduced against him at trial if he commits a serious offense against person or property is too speculative a hypothesis to support this claim."
  Hate crimes have a tendency to be bloodier or uglier than simple robberies or assaults. Individuals have a right to freedom from random acts of violence. Society has the right to punish those acts.
 Some people would have us believe that the thought police will start throwing all the Jerry Fallwells and Pat Robertsonsof the world in jail because they have some rather negative views on homosexuals.
 If I am hit by a meteor tomorrow and I can prove that it was the result of this opinion piece, then Robertson is in serious trouble. Otherwise, I do not think that even the most rabid homophobe has anything to worry about. We still have the First Amendment in this country.
 If you act out your beliefs, you'd better be careful, however. Hate crimes are crimes of violence. Burning a cross in your own yard while wearing a sheet is still legal in the United States. But crucifying a college student on a fence will land you in prison. And murder springing from bigotry should be dealt with harshly indeed.
 This scourge of hate is deeply imbedded in American life. I see it much more often than I care to. Hate-crime laws help at the very least as an example to our children.
 I am not so naive as to think they are a real deterence to a bigot. But they do send the message that we as a society condemn crime based on race,religion and sexual orientation. It is the least we can do.
Eric Parish lives in Vista.

Oceanside North County Times   December 19, 1998
'Tis the season to be heartless
Eric Parish
Community Forum
 Thank you Vista City Council, for voting to open up a winter shelter in the old Broadway shopping center. Anyone who has tried sleeping outdoors the last few weeks knows that our sunny Southern California winters are great - as long as you have a roof over your head. Otherwise it can get seriously cold and wet out there at night.
 I have lived on the street before and understand firsthand the importance of having a place to take a shower and having somewhere warm and dry to sleep.
 I want to dispel the myth I have been reading in this paper that the homeless are one big party-time, malt-liquor chugging, Dumpster-diving group of fun-loving guys who have chosen their homelessness as a happy-go-lucky lifestyle.
 This characterization is not used just by ignorant Scrooges or the cold-hearted businessmen who want their neighborhoods to look more upscale. Some of our city councilors believe this nonsense. Even some who actually work with the homeless make a distinction between homeless families with children and the more hard-core men with drug and alcohol problems, not to mention the mentally ill. Does focusing only on the families make the more hardcore homeless go away?
 It seems we have abandoned the mentally ill to the streets or to the jails. By definition, they cannot gain our sympathy like a homeless waif can.
 The homeless men have a slightly better chance. The Alpha Project here in Vista comes to mind as an outstanding example of a helping hand for these guys. People who live on the street do respond to a fresh start, as the Alpha Project managers understand.
 Being homeless is not a permanent condition. It is mainly a matter of just plain ordinary bad luck - losing a job at the wrong time, breaking up with your wife. It can and does happen to anyone. People work hard to get out of this condition.
 Getting your life back together is a full-time job for the homeless, not some kind of drunken vacation. Locking the Dumpsters, closing the free meals at our local churches and having no winter shelter at all is what we hear every year about this time from some of our so-called community "leaders". Move all of them to some other town where the "pickings are easy".
 Ironically, this complaining about the homeless comes as we prepare to celebrate the birthday of a man who had much to say about compassion : "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you." Merry Christmas to all, especially the Vista City Council.
Eric Parish lives in Vista.
